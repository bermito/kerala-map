Kerala’s place in coffee

Kerala is India’s second-largest coffee-producing state, with Robusta making up much of its crop. Its farms contribute to an Indian coffee industry with a substantial export trade. Behind those production figures are growers whose names, methods and individual coffees deserve to be better known. [1] [2]

Specialty coffee gives people a way to get to know that work: through a named farm, a carefully processed lot or a cup served with an explanation of where it came from. In Kozhikode, also known as Calicut, cafés, roasters and community gatherings have made those conversations part of the local coffee culture.

Arayie and the early community

Arayie The Earth Store, near Kozhikode’s beach, is an important starting point in the local history shared with this journal. That account remembers it as Kerala’s first dedicated specialty-coffee serving space and an early meeting place for the city’s pandemic-era coffee community. [Local history]

A dedicated coffee space allows a different kind of visit. There is time to ask about the beans, compare brewing methods and understand why one cup tastes different from another. Such conversations give people a reason to return and something to share with friends.

Roasting in Kozhikode

Bermito Coffee Roasters, founded by brothers Vajid and Hadi Roshan, roasts in Kozhikode. Caffeine Letter’s Indian roaster directory lists it as Kerala’s first specialty coffee roastery. [3] [4]

Roasting locally brings another part of the coffee’s preparation closer to the people drinking it. The questions become more specific: who grew this lot, how was it processed, and what is the roaster trying to bring out in the cup? Bermito’s work with Kerala-grown coffees, including Excelsa from Terra Vagamon, makes that connection tangible.

Koyan Althani and Urban Hippie Coffee Roasters are also part of Kozhikode’s roasting community. Urban Hippie’s biography records his experience in farming, processing, cupping and roasting, and a first runner-up finish in an Indian coffee roasting championship. His work connects an understanding of the crop with the skills needed to roast and serve it. [5]

Sukoru, Southwave and Underblu

Sukoru Specialty Coffee & Concept Store, founded by Marshad, describes itself as Kerala’s first specialty coffee and concept store. Alongside coffee and design, it has provided a venue for people to meet around Kerala-grown coffees, including Kerala Origins’ first public gathering. [6] [12]

Southwave places coffee alongside fashion, art and music. Its Calicut experience store opened in June 2024, with Under Blu described in its history as the café within the store. [7]

Underblu’s coffee gatherings also drew coverage in The New Indian Express. They show how coffee can become part of a city’s social life: a reason to meet, listen to music and spend time with people beyond a routine café visit. [8]

Brewing together

The local account shared with this journal credits Underblu Specialty Coffee and Muhammad Rafeeque, known as Putt, with hosting Kerala’s first AeroPress championship in Calicut. [Local history]

The Indian AeroPress Championship has also listed a Calicut regional round at The Long House Collective. [9]

Competitions give home brewers and professionals a shared task. They prepare recipes, taste the results and learn from each other. The value carries into everyday brewing long after a winner is announced.

Kerala Origins and the producers

Kerala Origins was founded on 25 June 2026 to bring producer knowledge and roasting experience together. Its purpose is to help Kerala’s coffees gain recognition through tasting, practical support for producers and connections with roasters and buyers. [10]

The four co-founders are:

  • Vajid Kaitha Valappil — Bermito Coffee Roasters
  • Ephrem Joseph — Terra Vagamon Conservancy
  • Moideen Kalladi — Silent Valley Estate
  • Nihal Parambath — On Ground Farm

Its first public gathering took place at Sukoru in Kozhikode on 11 July 2026. The Kochi edition followed at Cochin Club, Fort Kochi, on 10 October 2026. Coffees listed for the first gathering came from Terra Vagamon, On Ground, Silent Valley, Puliyarmala and Windermere Estate. [11] [12]

These gatherings put the producer within reach of the person tasting the coffee. Questions about harvesting, fermentation and drying become conversations with people who do that work. For a visitor, the farm name on a bag can become someone they have met.

A closer look at Excelsa and Mara Kaapi

Bermito’s Terra Vagamon Excelsa Anoxic Naturals is one example of this attention to Kerala-grown coffee. The product identifies Vagamon as its origin and describes a 120-hour anoxic fermentation. The roaster’s notes include clove, plum and molasses. [13]

Those details give drinkers something specific to explore: a producer, a species and a process. They also encourage a fresh look at coffees that may be familiar locally but less familiar on specialty menus.

Bermito’s journal makes a related case for Mara Kaapi, describing the tree coffee’s association with older estates and boundary planting. [14] The names should remain clear: Excelsa and Libex are different. The scientific study published in 2026 identifies Coffea × libex as a Liberica–Excelsa hybrid. The local name Mara Kaapi alone cannot identify every tree or coffee lot. [15]

Coffee gatherings across Kerala

Cuppings, brewing competitions and festivals give Kerala’s coffee community places to meet beyond the café counter. Kerala Coffee Festival is one such initiative, centred on roasters, cafés, brewers and coffee drinkers. [16]

The purpose of these gatherings is lasting: to make different coffees easier to discover and give people an opportunity to learn by tasting together. Our Kerala coffee festivals and events guide explores these different ways to take part.

Follow the coffee back to its producer

Kerala’s coffee reputation is built through individual coffees and the people behind them. A bag that names its producer, a roaster who explains the process and a café that credits its suppliers all help customers understand what they are drinking.

Kozhikode offers several places to begin that exploration. Its cafés and roasteries connect with gatherings in Kochi and with producers across Kerala. The relationship between them is what this map sets out to document.

Explore farms and producers, find the roasters working with their coffee, and look for a café or shop where you can taste it or buy a bag. Growing, roasting and serving are different parts of the work; a Kerala roaster may use beans from several regions. Asking for the producer’s name is a useful place to start.
